# Noise reduction

`reduceNoise(gpu, source, signal)` reduces a photo's noise once, at three strengths, before its adjustments: a RAW photo on its Bayer mosaic before the GPU demosaics it, any other image in its encoded color. It runs without React, model weights, or workers, entirely in WGSL but for small fits in TypeScript. The source keeps the result until it closes, and each renderer composes it at the image layer's luminance and color strengths in one pass, so moving either slider, or editing any layer above, never reduces noise again.

1. **Noise model, RAW.** Every 16 × 16 block of sensor pixels gives each cell position its mean and the variance of its diagonal Haar detail, which flat blocks owe to noise alone. Over 40 equal-count bins along the signal, the quietest tenth of each bin gives a point, and a weighted line through them is the position's Poisson-Gaussian model, variance = gain · signal + floor. Texture only adds variance, so the fit runs again without points more than 30 % above the line, which follows the noise where a detailed photo has few flat blocks. Positions fit apart: some cameras scale a channel digitally, which scales its noise too.
2. **Spectrum.** For a RAW photo, the generalized Anscombe transform, measured from its value at black so half floats stay precise, makes each position's noise unit Gaussian, and an orthonormal transform turns red, the two greens, and blue into light, green against magenta, red against blue, and the greens' difference. Samples below the transform's lowest value, such as dead pixels, are clamped there. Any other image is filtered in the sRGB transfer's encoding, where noise varies far less with light than in linear, as light and two color differences.
3. **Pyramid.** Averaging 2 × 2 texels builds up to six levels, down to 32 texels. Each level measures each component's noise from its flattest blocks. For a RAW photo, the finest level's noise is the model's, which a lower measure corrects; for an image, its measure, never below 8-bit rounding. A coarser level takes its own measure only up to 15 % over what white noise would leave there: lossy compression correlates noise and raises it at coarse levels, while texture, which a coarse level's flattest blocks still hold, raises a measure far more.
4. **Filter.** From the coarsest level up, sliding 8 × 8 DCT denoising runs in two steps: hard thresholding at 3 deviations, 3.5 for the greens' difference, then Wiener shrinkage by the first estimate, with color shrinking twice and the greens' difference eight times as hard as light. Every patch position counts, mirrored past the image's edges so edges and corners get as many as the rest; each output texel gathers the 64 patches over it in workgroup memory, weighted by how little of each survived and by a Kaiser window. A patch's DC stays whole, so every level stays unbiased, except the greens' difference: both greens see the same light, so its mean is noise or imbalance, which demosaicing would draw as a maze. After each step, the upsampled difference between the coarser estimate and the step's own 2 × 2 averages, blurred at the coarse size, replaces the step's low frequencies: broad noise the 8 × 8 patches cannot tell from the image goes, the coarser level's finest frequencies, where its patches ring at edges, stay out, and where the two levels agree nothing changes.
5. **Anchors.** The filter runs at three strengths, 0.35, 0.7, and 1.4 times the measured noise, sharing the pyramid and its measures. For a RAW photo each keeps, through the closed-form inverse that is unbiased for Poisson-Gaussian noise (Mäkitalo and Foi), its change to the decoded samples; for an image, its light at full size and its color at half, which a reduced image's color needs no more of.
6. **Composing.** An amount from 0 to 100 scales the filtered noise linearly: 25, 50, and 100 reach the anchors, and an amount between two blends them, or the weakest with the photo as decoded. Luminance weighs the light component, color the two color differences, and the stronger of the two the greens' difference. For a RAW photo, each anchor's change splits into those components along the transform's slope where the strongest anchor puts each sample, so the split follows the filter's, and the weighted parts add to the decoded samples: an anchor at both strengths gives that anchor exactly, and 0 the samples as decoded.

Patches are filtered alone. Grouping similar ones, as BM3D does, ranks candidates by a distance that noise dominates in dark areas; along edges it groups aligned patches whose average keeps a profile of noise, which showed as streaks. Hot pixels are left alone: telling one from a fine highlight by its neighbors removed far more detail from sharp photos than it saved.

On synthetic RAW mosaics made from a sharp photo, at three noise levels, an amount near 50 comes closest to the clean photo, 2 to 3.5 dB nearer than the noisy one; 100 smooths further, which very noisy photos may want. An image with added sRGB noise gains 5 to 7 dB near 50.

## Limits

Detail with less signal than noise is gone for any filter: in the darkest areas of a high-ISO photo, strong amounts leave smooth rather than textured surfaces. Very fine repeated texture near the noise level softens. A detailed photo with little noise can still measure more noise than it has, which a lower amount corrects. The RAW model assumes Poisson-Gaussian noise; row or column banding, which some sensors add, passes through. An image's noise has no model, and JPEG block edges are not noise. X-Trans and linear DNG files have no noise reduction. An image reduces at full size in three components, so it costs about four times a RAW photo of as many pixels.

## Verification

`tests/noise-reduction.test.ts` checks the noise fit against blocks of known noise, a third of them textured, and that amounts scale the filtered noise linearly. `tests/noise-reduction.e2e.ts` opens a RAW fixture with seeded Poisson-Gaussian noise and a noisy PNG, checks that their noise falls well below the original while their mean holds, that color reduces apart from light, and that 0 gives back the decoded pixels.

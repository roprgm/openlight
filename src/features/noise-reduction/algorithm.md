# Noise reduction

`reduceNoise(gpu, source, signal)` reduces a photo's noise once, before its adjustments: a RAW photo on its Bayer mosaic before the GPU demosaics it, any other image in its encoded color. It runs without React, model weights, or workers, entirely in WGSL but for small fits in TypeScript. The source keeps the result until it closes, and each renderer composes it at the image layer's luminance and color strengths in one pass, so moving either slider, or editing any layer above, never reduces noise again.

1. **Noise model, RAW.** Every 16 × 16 block of sensor pixels gives each cell position its mean and the variance of its diagonal Haar detail, which flat blocks owe to noise alone. Over 40 equal-count bins along the signal, the quietest tenth of each bin gives a point, and a weighted line through them is the position's Poisson-Gaussian model, variance = gain · signal + floor. Texture only adds variance, so the fit runs again without points more than 30 % above the line, which follows the noise where a detailed photo has few flat blocks. Positions fit apart: some cameras scale a channel digitally, which scales its noise too.
2. **Spectrum.** For a RAW photo, the generalized Anscombe transform, measured from its value at black so half floats stay precise, makes each position's noise unit Gaussian, and an orthonormal transform turns red, the two greens, and blue into light, green against magenta, red against blue, and the greens' difference. Samples below the transform's lowest value, such as dead pixels, are clamped there. Any other image is filtered in the sRGB transfer's encoding, where noise varies far less with light than in linear, as light and two color differences, each at half size and apart: light as each 2 × 2 cell's Haar transform, its mean and three differences, and color as each cell's sum. Every spectrum then takes 8 bytes per 2 × 2 pixels, so the largest texture a device takes fits one storage binding.
3. **Pyramid.** Averaging 2 × 2 texels builds up to six levels, down to 32 texels. Each level measures each component's noise from its flattest blocks. For a RAW photo, the finest level's noise is the model's, which a lower measure corrects. For an image, never below 8-bit rounding, light takes the least of its four measures that find any noise, since white noise holds the same deviation in each and texture raises a coarse cell's mean most, and each color difference its own. A coarser level takes its own measure only up to 15 % over what white noise would leave there: lossy compression correlates noise and raises it at coarse levels, while texture, which a coarse level's flattest blocks still hold, raises a measure far more.
4. **Filter.** From the coarsest level up, sliding 8 × 8 DCT denoising runs in two steps: hard thresholding at 3 deviations, 3.5 for the greens' difference, then Wiener shrinkage by the first estimate, with color shrinking twice and the greens' difference eight times as hard as light. Patches start on every other texel, mirrored past the image's edges so edges and corners get as many as the rest; each output texel gathers the 16 patches over it in workgroup memory, weighted by how little of each survived and by a Kaiser window. That is a quarter of the patches of every texel's, within 0.1 dB of them on test photos. A patch's DC stays whole where a component holds a level, so every level stays unbiased, but not for the greens' difference: both greens see the same light, so its mean is noise or imbalance, which demosaicing would draw as a maze. After each step, the upsampled difference between the coarser estimate and the step's own 2 × 2 averages, blurred at the coarse size, replaces the step's low frequencies: broad noise the 8 × 8 patches cannot tell from the image goes, the coarser level's finest frequencies, where its patches ring at edges, stay out, and where the two levels agree nothing changes.
5. **Reduction.** The filter runs once, at the measured noise. For a RAW photo it keeps, through the closed-form inverse that is unbiased for Poisson-Gaussian noise (Mäkitalo and Foi), its change to the decoded samples; for an image, its light at full size and its color at half, which a reduced image's color needs no more of.
6. **Composing.** An amount from 0 to 100 sets how much of each component's difference between the photo and its reduction goes, in noise deviations: none at 0, all at 100. Between, a component loses nearly all of a difference whose 3 × 3 neighborhood varies by less than a limit, and about the limit's worth of one that varies more: noise, which varies by one deviation, goes evenly, while detail, which varies more, mostly stays. Weighing neighborhoods rather than single values keeps the few noise values that stand out from staying as dots. At an amount a, the limit is a / (100 − a) times its value at 50: one deviation for light, three for color, which holds little detail, and 0.7 for the greens' difference, which the filter shrinks hardest. Luminance sets light, color the two color differences, and the stronger of the two the greens' difference. For a RAW photo, differences count along the transform's slope where the reduction puts each sample, as the filter saw them.

Patches are filtered alone. Grouping similar ones, as BM3D does, ranks candidates by a distance that noise dominates in dark areas; along edges it groups aligned patches whose average keeps a profile of noise, which showed as streaks. Hot pixels are left alone: telling one from a fine highlight by its neighbors removed far more detail from sharp photos than it saved.

On synthetic RAW mosaics made from a sharp photo, at three noise levels, an amount near 50 comes closest to the clean photo, 2 to 3 dB nearer than the noisy one, within 0.3 dB of the best a single strength reaches; 100, the whole reduction, smooths further, which very noisy photos may want. An image with added sRGB noise gains 5 to 6 dB near 50.

## Cost

Reducing runs the pyramid once. At its peak, the finest level's noisy spectrum, its two step estimates, the coarser estimate, and the blur between them take about 8 bytes per pixel; coarser levels free as soon as they are filtered, and an image's color waits through light's at 1 byte per pixel. Afterwards a RAW photo keeps 2 bytes per pixel for its change and 2 for its decoded samples, and each renderer 2 for its composed samples, besides the development it already makes for a white balance. An image keeps 3 bytes per pixel for its light and color, and each renderer an image in the source's own format: 4 bytes per pixel for 8-bit sources, dithered so smooth reductions don't band.

## Limits

Detail with less signal than noise is gone for any filter: in the darkest areas of a high-ISO photo, strong amounts leave smooth rather than textured surfaces. Very fine repeated texture near the noise level softens. A detailed photo with little noise can still measure more noise than it has, which a lower amount corrects. The RAW model assumes Poisson-Gaussian noise; row or column banding, which some sensors add, passes through. An image's noise has no model, and JPEG block edges are not noise. Filtering an image's light through 2 × 2 Haar components, which keeps it at 2 bytes per pixel, comes about 0.6 dB short of filtering it at full size. X-Trans and linear DNG files have no noise reduction.

## Verification

`tests/noise-reduction.test.ts` checks the noise fit against blocks of known noise, a third of them textured. `tests/noise-reduction.e2e.ts` opens a RAW fixture with seeded Poisson-Gaussian noise and a noisy PNG, checks that their noise falls well below the original while their mean holds, that color reduces apart from light and keeps the image's color, and that 0 gives back the decoded pixels.
